# Atrial Fibrillation: Diagnosis and Management

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia,
affecting approximately 33 million individuals worldwide. Its prevalence
increases with age, reaching approximately 10% in adults over 80 years.

## Pathophysiology

The hallmark of AF is chaotic, rapid atrial electrical activity resulting in
irregular ventricular response. Multiple re-entrant wavelets propagate
simultaneously through the atrial myocardium. Heart rate typically ranges from
100-160 bpm during untreated AF.

Key pathological mechanisms include:
- Electrical remodelling: shortening of the atrial refractory period
- Structural remodelling: atrial fibrosis and dilation
- Autonomic modulation: sympathetic and parasympathetic triggers

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed by 12-lead ECG showing irregularly irregular rhythm
with absence of distinct P waves. The QRS complex is usually narrow (<120 ms)
unless aberrant conduction or bundle branch block exists.

Diagnostic criteria:
- Irregular RR intervals with no distinct P waves
- Atrial rate 350-600 impulses per minute
- Ventricular rate typically 100-160 bpm (untreated)

Holter monitoring (24-48 hour ambulatory ECG) or implantable loop recorders
are used to detect paroxysmal AF when standard ECG is non-diagnostic.

## Risk Stratification

The CHA2DS2-VASc score guides anticoagulation decisions:
- C: Congestive heart failure (1 point)
- H: Hypertension (1 point)
- A2: Age >= 75 years (2 points)
- D: Diabetes mellitus (1 point)
- S2: Stroke/TIA/thromboembolism (2 points)
- V: Vascular disease (1 point)
- A: Age 65-74 years (1 point)
- Sc: Sex category female (1 point)

Score >= 2 in males or >= 3 in females: anticoagulation strongly recommended.

Bleeding risk is assessed with the HAS-BLED score. A high HAS-BLED score
(>= 3) should prompt correction of modifiable bleeding risk factors, not
withholding of anticoagulation.

## Treatment

### Rate Control

Rate control is the initial strategy in most patients. Target resting
heart rate is <110 bpm (lenient) or <80 bpm (strict, selected patients).

Agents:
- Beta-blockers: metoprolol succinate 25-200 mg/day, bisoprolol 2.5-10 mg/day
- Non-dihydropyridine CCBs: diltiazem 120-360 mg/day, verapamil 120-480 mg/day
- Digoxin: 0.125-0.25 mg/day (primarily in sedentary patients with HFrEF)

### Rhythm Control

Rhythm control is preferred in symptomatic patients, younger patients, and
those with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).

Antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs):
- Flecainide 100-200 mg BID (structurally normal heart only)
- Propafenone 150-300 mg TID (structurally normal heart only)
- Amiodarone 200 mg/day maintenance (most effective, most side effects)
- Dronedarone 400 mg BID (avoid in permanent AF or severe HF)

Catheter ablation (pulmonary vein isolation, PVI) achieves rhythm control
in 60-80% of patients at 1 year and is preferred over AADs in many centres.

### Anticoagulation

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are preferred over warfarin:
- Apixaban 5 mg BID (or 2.5 mg BID if >= 2 of: age >= 80, weight <= 60 kg, creatinine >= 1.5 mg/dL)
- Rivaroxaban 20 mg OD with evening meal
- Dabigatran 150 mg BID (or 110 mg BID in selected patients)
- Edoxaban 60 mg OD (or 30 mg OD if CrCl 15-50 mL/min, weight <= 60 kg)

Warfarin (target INR 2.0-3.0) remains appropriate when DOACs are unavailable,
in patients with mechanical heart valves, or in moderate-to-severe mitral
stenosis.

## Prognosis

AF significantly increases the risk of:
- Stroke: 5-fold increase; 20-30% of all strokes are AF-related
- Heart failure: bidirectional relationship; AF precipitates and worsens HF
- Dementia: independent risk factor
- Mortality: 2-fold increase in age-adjusted mortality

With appropriate anticoagulation, stroke risk is reduced by 60-70%.
Early rhythm control (initiated within 1 year of diagnosis) is associated
with improved cardiovascular outcomes in selected patients (EAST-AFNET 4 trial).
