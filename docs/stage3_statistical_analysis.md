## 1. Objective & Hypotheses

### Primary Research Question
In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems for clinical cardiovascular medicine, conventional information retrieval (IR) metrics—such as Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG), Precision, and Recall—are standardly used to evaluate retrieval systems. However, it remains an open empirical question whether higher performance on these IR ranking benchmarks translates reliably into:
1. **Evidence sufficiency** (whether the retrieved passages contain the critical clinical facts necessary to address the query), and
2. **Downstream answer quality** (whether the generative model produces answers that are grounded, factual, and relevant to the clinical question).

### Hypotheses
- **Hypothesis 1 (H1):** Higher conventional retrieval quality (measured by NDCG@K, Precision@K, and Recall@K) is generally associated with higher downstream evidence coverage.
- **Hypothesis 2 (H2):** Downstream evidence coverage exhibits a substantially stronger association with downstream answer quality (Composite Quality) than conventional retrieval metrics alone.

### Important Methodological Boundaries & Claim Restrictions
- **Automated Answer Quality:** Downstream evaluation was performed using an automated, independent LLM judge (Cohere `command-r-08-2024`, temperature $0.0$, strictly blind to system identities and ranking metrics). The evaluator scores quantify **automated answer fidelity and alignment relative to reference evidence**, NOT absolute clinical correctness, medical safety, expert consensus, or diagnostic validity.
- **No Causal Inference:** All reported statistics are correlational and comparative within a controlled empirical evaluation matrix. Correlation does not imply causation.
- **Prominent Shared-Reference Limitation:**  
  > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Shared-Reference Dependency Disclosure:**  
  > Because both retrieval evaluation (qrels relevance grades) and downstream evidence coverage rely on the same qrels-derived grade-2 reference evidence passages, the relationship between conventional retrieval metrics and evidence coverage must be interpreted as a **shared-reference association** rather than an independent external validation of retrieval metrics.

## 4. Statistical Methodology

1. **Unit of Analysis:**  
   The primary inferential unit is the **query-level observation within each of the 15 experimental configurations ($n = 59$)**. Naive pooling of all 885 conditions into a single correlation is avoided as a primary analysis because repeated evaluations across the same 59 queries violate independence assumptions.
2. **Correlation Measure:**  
   Spearman rank correlation coefficient ($\rho$) with two-sided $p$-values. Effect sizes and directions are emphasized over thresholded $p$-values.
3. **H2 Paired Bootstrap Comparison:**  
   To evaluate whether Evidence Coverage has a stronger association with Composite Quality than NDCG@K ($\rho(\text{Coverage}, \text{Composite}) > \rho(\text{NDCG}, \text{Composite})$), a paired bootstrap test was conducted:
   - For each configuration, the 59 query IDs were resampled with replacement for $B = 2,000$ iterations (fixed seed: $42$).
   - The bootstrap difference $\Delta_b = \rho_b(\text{Coverage}, \text{Composite}) - \rho_b(\text{NDCG}, \text{Composite})$ was calculated for each iteration.
   - We report the observed difference, the 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval, and the empirical proportion $P(\Delta > 0)$.
4. **Paired Retriever Comparisons:**  
   Because all retrievers evaluate the identical 59 queries, paired comparisons at fixed $K$ were evaluated using the non-parametric **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**, accompanied by effect size ($r = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{N}}$) and **Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR)** multiple-comparison corrections.
5. **System-Level Exploratory Analysis:**  
   Correlations across configuration-level means ($n = 15$) are reported descriptively and explicitly labeled as exploratory.
6. **LSH Robustness:**  
   Because LSH exhibits extreme retrieval floor effects (NDCG $\approx 0.07$), all analyses are reported for both the complete 5-system matrix ($n = 15$) and the non-LSH competitive subset ($n = 12$).

## 7. Hypothesis 1 (H1) Results

> **H1 Prediction:** Higher conventional retrieval quality is generally associated with higher downstream evidence coverage.

### Empirical Findings
1. **Direction:** Across all 15 configurations, the Spearman rank correlation between NDCG@K and Evidence Coverage is **strictly positive** ($\rho > 0$).
2. **Statistical Significance:** 13 of the 15 configurations demonstrate statistically significant positive correlations ($p < 0.05$). The only two non-significant configurations are at $K=20$ (Dense $K=20$: $\rho = +0.213, p = 0.105$; BM25+Dense+LSH RRF $K=20$: $\rho = +0.224, p = 0.088$).
3. **Magnitude in Competitive Systems:** For the four viable retrieval architectures (BM25, Dense, BM25+Dense RRF, and BM25+Dense+LSH RRF), the association is **modest to moderate** (mean $\rho = +0.336$, median $\rho = +0.335$, range $+0.213$ to $+0.447$).
4. **Inflation by Retrieval Floor Effect:** For LSH, the correlation is substantially higher ($\rho = +0.726$ to $+0.791$). This strong correlation is driven by zero-hit floor effects: when LSH retrieves zero relevant passages, both NDCG and Evidence Coverage collapse to zero simultaneously.
5. **Decay with Cutoff Depth:** As $K$ increases from 5 to 10 to 20, the correlation between NDCG and Evidence Coverage consistently attenuates across systems (e.g., BM25: $0.447 \to 0.417 \to 0.321$; Dense: $0.348 \to 0.364 \to 0.213$; Hybrid: $0.433 \to 0.308 \to 0.224$).

### H1 Decision: **SUPPORTED**
The empirical evidence supports Hypothesis 1: higher conventional retrieval quality is positively associated with higher evidence coverage. However, the strength of this relationship is modest among viable retrieval engines and decays at deeper cutoff depths.

## 10. Cutoff Depth ($K$) Dynamics

An essential question in clinical RAG is whether expanding retrieval context ($K=5 \to 10 \to 20$) improves downstream answer quality.

### Downstream vs. Retrieval Trajectory
1. **Retrieval Recall Surges:** Across all systems, Recall@K increases markedly from $K=5$ to $K=20$ (e.g., BM25 recall increases from $0.236 \to 0.385 \to 0.554$; Dense recall increases from $0.187 \to 0.284 \to 0.448$).
2. **Downstream Quality Peaks at $K=10$:** Downstream Composite Quality peaks at $K=10$ and does NOT improve further at $K=20$:
   - BM25 Composite Quality: $0.7805 \ (K=5) \to \mathbf{0.8184} \ (K=10) \to 0.8167 \ (K=20)$.
   - Dense Composite Quality: $0.8099 \ (K=5) \to \mathbf{0.8124} \ (K=10) \to 0.8105 \ (K=20)$.
   - BM25+Dense RRF Composite Quality: $0.8184 \ (K=5) \to \mathbf{0.8181} \ (K=10) \to 0.8127 \ (K=20)$.
3. **Factual Consistency Diminishes at $K=20$:** While Evidence Coverage slightly rises or plateaus, Factual Consistency systematically declines from $K=10$ to $K=20$ across all viable systems:
   - BM25 Consistency: $0.8271 \ (K=10) \to 0.8246 \ (K=20)$.
   - Dense Consistency: $0.8195 \ (K=10) \to 0.8153 \ (K=20)$.
   - BM25+Dense RRF Consistency: $0.8229 \ (K=10) \to 0.8153 \ (K=20)$.
4. **Context Dilution & Distractor Effects:** Expanding the context window from 10 to 20 passages introduces lower-ranked, peripheral passages that introduce distractor facts and dilute the generator's focus without providing additional core evidence.

LSH is an extreme retrieval outlier in this benchmark (NDCG@10 = $0.073$, Recall@10 = $0.049$). Including or excluding LSH radically alters system-level conclusions.

- LSH acts as an extreme bimodal anchor. When LSH is included, the artificial contrast between viable systems (NDCG $\sim 0.50$, Composite $\sim 0.81$) and LSH (NDCG $\sim 0.07$, Composite $\sim 0.40$) generates an apparent system-level correlation ($\rho \approx 0.52, p < 0.05$).

## 13. Comprehensive Statistical Limitations

1. **Shared-Reference Dependency:** As disclosed, qrels-derived grade-2 reference evidence was used to compute both retrieval metrics and downstream evidence coverage. They are not fully independent measurements.
2. **Automated LLM Judge:** Evaluator scores were produced by Cohere `command-r-08-2024` with structured schema decoding. While strict blindness and cross-family independence were maintained, automated LLM evaluation is not a substitute for clinical expert adjudication.
3. **Absence of Independent Gold Answers:** Reference evidence consists of passage excerpts rather than human clinician reference answers.
4. **Corpus & Domain Specificity:** The study used a single cardiovascular guideline corpus (1,142 chunks) and 59 primary clinical queries. Generalization to other medical corpora cannot be claimed without external validation.
5. **No Causal Inference:** Retrieval interventions and downstream answer scores are observational associations within this experimental matrix.

## 14. Synthesis & Research Interpretation

Responding to the core research questions:

1. **Does higher retrieval quality correspond to higher evidence coverage?**  
   *Yes (H1 Supported).* Query-level correlations are positive in 100% of configurations ($\rho \approx +0.21 \text{ to } +0.45$ for competitive systems).
2. **Does evidence coverage correspond to better downstream answer quality?**  
   *Yes, overwhelmingly.* Evidence coverage correlates with composite quality at $\rho \approx +0.87 \text{ to } +0.99$ across all configurations.
3. **Is the association between evidence coverage and answer quality stronger than conventional retrieval metrics?**  
   *Yes, significantly (H2 Strongly Supported).* In 100% of configurations and 100% of bootstrap iterations ($P(\Delta > 0) = 1.000$), Evidence Coverage has a markedly stronger association with answer quality than NDCG.
4. **Does BM25's conventional retrieval dominance translate downstream?**  
   *No.* Dense achieves downstream parity at $K=10$ and outperforms BM25 at $K=5$, despite substantially lower conventional retrieval scores.
5. **Does hybrid retrieval improve downstream quality?**  
   *Yes.* BM25+Dense RRF consistently provides top-tier composite quality ($0.818$) across cutoffs.
6. **Does increasing $K$ improve downstream quality?**  
   *Only up to $K=10$.* $K=20$ substantially increases recall but fails to improve answer quality, slightly degrading factual consistency.
7. **What happens when LSH is included vs. excluded?**  
   *LSH inflates system-level correlations.* Removing LSH reveals that within viable systems, NDCG differences do not predict downstream answer quality.
8. **Summary of Hypotheses:**  
   - **H1:** Supported (moderate correlation).  
   - **H2:** Strongly Supported (statistically robust and consistent across all conditions).

---

## 15. Conclusions

1. **Conventional IR metrics are insufficient sole proxies for downstream clinical RAG quality.** While they detect catastrophic retrieval failure (e.g., LSH), they fail to predict downstream answer quality among viable sparse and dense retrieval systems.
2. **Evidence coverage is the critical mediating bottleneck.** Downstream generative quality is governed primarily by whether the retrieved context contains the requisite clinical facts, not by ranking position or lexical precision alone.
3. **Dense retrieval achieves high downstream efficacy with compact context.** Dense vector embeddings capture semantic clinical nuance that enables generators to achieve high answer quality at lower cutoffs ($K=5$) compared to BM25.
4. **$K=10$ represents the optimal operational cutoff.** Context expansion beyond $K=10$ exhibits diminishing returns and risks context pollution in cardiovascular clinical RAG.
